# Argument guidance

Read when developing a thesis or organizing a complex position.

## Choose a contribution

A Perspective may reconcile competing findings, reframe a problem, challenge an assumption, propose a conceptual distinction, or argue for a research or practice agenda. Its contribution should be identifiable beyond summarizing a topic.

Make the thesis specific enough that a knowledgeable reader can assess it. Explain the reasoning connecting the evidence to that thesis. Avoid implying exhaustive coverage when the work is a selective scholarly argument.

## Test the reasoning

Distinguish observation from interpretation and value judgments. Examine alternative explanations, counterexamples, boundary conditions, and consequences that would follow if the thesis were wrong.

Represent opposing positions in their strongest relevant form. Acknowledge evidence that narrows the thesis, even when it makes the argument less sweeping. A cautious, well-supported interpretation is preferable to a novelty claim based on an incomplete search.

## Develop the piece

Let the structure follow the argument. Give enough background for the intended reader, then use examples and evidence to advance the position. Recommendations should follow from the reasoning and make their assumptions, tradeoffs, and limitations clear.

Where a venue specifies a format, verify and apply it. Avoid turning every Perspective into a systematic review or requiring experimental proof for explicitly qualified conceptual proposals.
