# Common L1 Interference Patterns

Non-native English features organized by L1 background. Flag these descriptively — their origin does not change their impact on the reader, but understanding the pattern helps produce more precise issue descriptions and directional corrections.

**Assessment principle**: Judge the output the same way regardless of the author's L1. An article error is an article error whether the author is a Chinese L1 or German L1 speaker. The L1 categories below help the assessor recognize systematic patterns rather than treating each occurrence as random.

---

## Chinese L1 (Mandarin / Cantonese)

### Article Omission or Overuse

Mandarin lacks a definite/indefinite article system. Common patterns:
- Missing "the" before specific referents: "Results of study showed..." → "The results of the study showed..."
- Missing "a/an" before first-mention countable nouns: "We conducted experiment..." → "We conducted an experiment..."
- Overuse of "the" with general concepts: "The diabetes is a common disease..." → "Diabetes is a common disease..."

### Subject-Verb Agreement with Third-Person Singular

Mandarin verbs do not inflect for person/number:
- "The model predict..." → "The model predicts..."
- "It have been shown..." → "It has been shown..."

### Plural Marker Omission

Mandarin does not mark plural morphologically:
- "Several study have..." → "Several studies have..."
- "All participant completed..." → "All participants completed..."

### Preposition Patterns

- "depend on" → not "depend from"
- "different from" → not "different with" (though "different than" is acceptable in American English)
- "related to" → not "related with"
- Missing prepositions in phrasal verbs: "We carried the experiment" → "We carried out the experiment"

### Word Order: Adverbial Placement

Mandarin places adverbials before the verb:
- "We in the hospital conducted a study..." → "We conducted a study in the hospital..."
- "The method accurately can predict..." → "The method can accurately predict..."

### Connective Overuse

- "Besides,..." used for addition where "Furthermore,..." or "In addition,..." would be more formal
- "On the other hand,..." used without a preceding "On the one hand,..." or where "However,..." is meant

### Redundant Paired Nouns

Under influence of Chinese bisyllabic/paired word preferences:
- "research studies" → "research" or "studies"
- "methods and approaches" → "methods" (if they are the same thing)
- "aims and objectives" → "aims" or "objectives"

### Register

- Tendency toward overly emphatic adverbs: "very important", "extremely significant", "absolutely necessary"
- Direct translations of Chinese academic formulaic expressions that read as overly assertive or informal in English

## Japanese / Korean L1

### Article Patterns

Similar to Chinese L1 — missing or overused articles.

### Subject Omission

Japanese/Korean allow subject-dropping:
- "Investigated the effect of..." (missing "We") → "We investigated the effect of..."

### Passive Overuse

Both languages use passive for objectivity more extensively than English academic style prefers (especially in Introduction and Discussion):
- "It was found by our study that..." → "We found that..." or "Our study found that..."

### Paragraph Structure

Japanese academic writing sometimes follows a "ki-shou-ten-ketsu" (introduction-development-turn-conclusion) structure where the "turn" introduces a seemingly unrelated idea. This can read as a non-sequitur in English academic prose.

## Romance Language L1 (Spanish, Portuguese, French, Italian)

### Cognate Overextension

Using Romance-language cognates with a different meaning or register in English:
- "actual" (meaning "current" in Romance) used where "current/present" is meant
- "eventually" (meaning "possibly" in Romance) used where "possibly/potentially" is meant
- "pretend" (meaning "intend" in Romance) used where "intend/aim" is meant

### Adjective Placement

Post-nominal adjectives common in Romance languages:
- "the patients diabetic" → "the diabetic patients"
- "the variables selected" → "the selected variables" (acceptable in both positions, but pre-nominal is the default)

### Preposition Transfer

- "depends of" (from Spanish "depende de", Portuguese "depende de") → "depends on"

### Sentence Length

Tendency toward longer, multi-clause sentences that are grammatically correct but strain English readability norms (> 35–40 words).

---

## Germanic Language L1 (German, Dutch, Scandinavian)

### Verb Position

German/Dutch SOV word order with V2 in main clauses can produce:
- "We have the patients into two groups divided" → "We divided the patients into two groups"

### Compound Nouns

Tendency to create long compound nouns where English prefers prepositional phrases:
- "the patient satisfaction improvement initiative" → acceptable; but strings of 4+ nouns become hard to parse

### Comma Usage

German comma rules are stricter than English; German L1 writers may use commas before all subordinate clauses where English would omit them:
- "We found, that the treatment was effective" → "We found that the treatment was effective"

---

## South Asian L1 (Hindi, Urdu, Tamil, Telugu, etc.)

### Progressive Aspect Overuse

Indian English uses progressive aspect more broadly than standard academic English:
- "We are finding that..." → "We find that..." or "We found that..."
- "The model is predicting..." → "The model predicts..."

### Article Patterns

Generally stronger article use than East Asian L1, but with differences in definite/indefinite choice:
- Overuse of "the" in general statements is common.

### Preposition Differences

- "discuss about" → "discuss" (no preposition)
- "comprise of" → "comprise" (no preposition; "consist of" is the alternative)

---

## Arabic L1

### Article Overuse

Arabic uses the definite article more extensively:
- "The science is important..." → "Science is important..."

### Relative Clause Structure

Arabic relative clauses often repeat the referent:
- "The patients that we included them..." → "The patients that we included..."

### Coordination Preference

Tendency toward paratactic structures (coordination with "and") where English prefers hypotactic structures (subordination):
- "We collected data and we analyzed it and we found..." → "After collecting data, we analyzed it and found..."

## General Non-Native Features (cross-L1)

### Hedging Patterns

- Over-hedging: multiple hedges stacking ("may possibly perhaps suggest...")
- Under-hedging: no modal verbs where academic convention expects them
- "It seems that..." in Results where a direct statement is more appropriate

### Formulaic Transfer

Direct translations of academic formulas from L1:
- "As everyone knows..." (from various L1 academic phrases meaning "as is well established")
- "According to the above-mentioned..." → "As described above..."
- "Last but not least..." → "Finally,..." (informal in STEM, acceptable in some humanities)

### Sentence-Initial Discourse Markers

Overuse of certain sentence-initial markers:
- "Meanwhile,..." used for any parallel point (should be used for simultaneous temporal events)
- "Moreover,..." / "Furthermore,..." / "In addition,..." used interchangeably where "Additionally,..." or a simple "Also,..." suffices
- "Besides,..." used as a general additive (informal; prefer "In addition,..." or "Furthermore,...")

---

## Assessment Usage

- Do not mention the author's presumed L1 in the assessment report unless the user has explicitly asked for L1-aware feedback.
- The pattern categories above are diagnostic tools for the assessor, not labels to apply to the manuscript.
- Flag the specific error descriptively (e.g., "missing definite article before specific referent"), not by L1 category (e.g., "Chinese L1 article error").
- Multiple patterns from a single L1 category strengthen the case that the issue is systematic, not random — use this to calibrate severity judgments.
