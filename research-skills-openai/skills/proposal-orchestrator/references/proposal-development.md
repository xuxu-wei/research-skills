# Proposal development guidance

Read when connecting the background argument, research aims, and work plan.

## Build an argument

A background can organize evidence by themes, move from established knowledge toward an unresolved problem, or combine these approaches. Choose the order that helps the intended reader understand why this work is needed.

Support factual claims with relevant evidence. Explain the gap's consequence and how the aims address it; the mere absence of prior work is not sufficient. Include evidence that limits or challenges the rationale.

Write the aims so that success would be interpretable. Distinguish an empirical question from a promised finding. Match the scale of the contribution to what the project can actually deliver.

## Make the work plan credible

For each aim, connect inputs, activities, analysis, and expected outputs. Show why the available measurements and comparisons support the intended inference. Discuss key failure risks and useful alternatives, including dependencies between aims.

Match the work to recruitment or data access, infrastructure, expertise, approvals, time, and budget. Identify estimates and evidence for them; do not supply fictional sample sizes, collaborations, access commitments, or ethical approvals.

A standalone SAP is useful when analysis decisions need sufficient detail to guide implementation or be fixed before results are examined. It is unnecessary for every early concept or nonquantitative proposal.

## Make the proposal readable

Use the call's required sections when there is a call. Otherwise choose a structure suited to the project. Define specialist terms where they matter, avoid repeated background, and make the question-to-method connection visible. Keep uncertainty and limitations in the argument instead of hiding them in an appendix.
