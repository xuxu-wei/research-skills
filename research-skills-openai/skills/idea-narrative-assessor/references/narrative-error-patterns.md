# Narrative error patterns

## Contents

Usage; defense and caveat patterns; workflow language; concept and reader
ordering; section function; gap framing; summary burden; conditional-extension
prominence; backtracking; additive-only repair; technically precise but
unreadable prose.

## How to use this reference

Complete an initial rubric-based assessment first. Then load only the entry matching an
observed category or a plausible false positive. The examples illustrate functions,
not reusable wording. Never diagnose from keyword counts, sentence length, or lexical
similarity to an example.

## Defense displaces motivation

- **Reader harm:** qualifications occupy the place where the problem, value, and
  contribution should be established.
- **Diagnostic:** removing defensive sentences reveals that the reader still cannot
  say what the study seeks to learn or why it matters.
- **Positive example:** the paragraph first states an unanswered problem and its
  consequence, then bounds the proposed contribution where that boundary becomes
  relevant.
- **Negative example:** the paragraph opens with a sequence of things the study will
  not prove and never develops a positive reason to conduct it.
- **False-positive boundary:** an early boundary is appropriate when it distinguishes
  the target question from a nearby but materially different question and directly
  enables the next reasoning step.

## Caveat saturation

- **Reader harm:** repeated qualifications fragment the main argument and obscure
  which statement is authoritative.
- **Diagnostic:** the same boundary appears in multiple sections without performing a
  new and necessary local function.
- **Positive example:** the complete limitation appears once in its authoritative
  section; the main text states only the scientifically bounded claim it needs.
- **Negative example:** summary, background, objectives, design, and interpretation
  restate the same caution in slightly different words.
- **False-positive boundary:** distinct constraints are not duplicates merely because
  they concern the same broad topic; compare their consequences and scope.

## Workflow language leakage

- **Reader harm:** implementation or review-state labels replace a scientific account
  of why a choice is made.
- **Diagnostic:** a phrase is meaningful mainly inside the production process and
  cannot be interpreted by the stated research audience without that process.
- **Positive example:** a dependency is described by the scientific condition that
  justifies proceeding.
- **Negative example:** the prose describes an internal approval state as though it
  were a property of the research question.
- **False-positive boundary:** a term also used in project management is acceptable
  when it has a stable scientific meaning in context and that meaning is the one used.

## Concept before problem

- **Reader harm:** readers must learn a construct before knowing what explanatory job
  it performs.
- **Diagnostic:** the construct arrives before the unresolved problem or observable
  phenomenon that motivates it.
- **Positive example:** the problem is established, then the construct is introduced
  as the needed representation or analytic device.
- **Negative example:** the opening defines several project-specific constructs before
  naming the research problem.
- **False-positive boundary:** a compact definition may come first when the construct
  is itself the study object and the definition immediately establishes the problem.

## Reader-baseline mismatch

- **Reader harm:** the intended audience must infer or look up concepts that the
  handoff says require explanation.
- **Diagnostic:** central reasoning depends on undeclared knowledge beyond the
  supplied assumed-known list.
- **Positive example:** familiar disciplinary concepts remain concise while the first
  cross-disciplinary dependency receives a plain first-use explanation.
- **Negative example:** every specialty is treated as if its readers share the other
  specialties' implicit vocabulary and conventions.
- **False-positive boundary:** technical terms need not be redefined when the reader
  handoff explicitly treats them as shared prior knowledge.

## Section-function failure

- **Reader harm:** headings promise an answer but deliver a different rhetorical task.
- **Diagnostic:** a section's content cannot answer the question implied by its
  heading without borrowing essential content from elsewhere.
- **Positive example:** a significance subsection states the consequence of resolving
  the gap for the declared audience.
- **Negative example:** a significance subsection merely repeats the proposed method.
- **False-positive boundary:** one paragraph may serve adjacent functions if both are
  explicit and the transition is intelligible; separation is a tool, not an end.

## Gap becomes novelty defense

- **Reader harm:** readers learn how the project differs from neighbors but not what
  remains unknown or unsupported.
- **Diagnostic:** the gap can be paraphrased only as “others have not used this exact
  combination.”
- **Positive example:** the gap names an unanswered scientific or evidential question;
  positioning is handled separately.
- **Negative example:** the gap consists entirely of distinctions from selected prior
  approaches.
- **False-positive boundary:** absence of an approach can constitute a gap when the
  missing capability is tied explicitly to an unanswered question and its consequence.

## Qualifier-stacked summary

- **Reader harm:** the main question and contribution disappear inside nested scope,
  conditions, and exceptions.
- **Diagnostic:** a careful reader cannot state the positive study aim after one pass,
  even though every individual qualifier may be accurate.
- **Positive example:** the summary states the bounded aim directly and reserves
  implementation detail for the relevant section.
- **Negative example:** the summary attempts to encode every contingency and failure
  interpretation before completing the aim.
- **False-positive boundary:** a technically dense summary is not defective when each
  qualifier changes the identity of the question and the declared readers can parse it
  without later definitions.

## Conditional extension crowds out the core

- **Reader harm:** a contingent or later component gains equal narrative weight to the
  declared main study because its full operating logic is repeated across required
  sections.
- **Diagnostic:** every required function can remain complete with one short,
  role-specific statement while one technical section retains the full logic.
- **Positive example:** the question, objective, evidence chain, output, and claim
  audit each state only their own relation to the contingent component; its method
  subsection contains the complete eligibility and operation rules.
- **Negative example:** the same eligibility, alternative, and interpretation boundary
  are re-explained in the abstract, question, objectives, work plan, methods, outputs,
  contribution, and claim table.
- **False-positive boundary:** repeated mention is not repeated exposition when each
  occurrence supplies a distinct required input, output, decision, or interpretation
  that cannot be recovered from the technical authority.

## Avoidable backtracking

- **Reader harm:** readers repeatedly move forward and backward to recover premises or
  definitions.
- **Diagnostic:** an earlier central statement depends on material introduced only
  later, without a deliberate preview that is understandable on its own.
- **Positive example:** a short bridge previews the dependency and the detail follows
  at the point of use.
- **Negative example:** the rationale relies on a category whose meaning is revealed
  only in a later technical section.
- **False-positive boundary:** forward references are acceptable when the earlier text
  is complete at its own level and the later material supplies optional depth.

## Additive-only repair

- **Reader harm:** every review cycle adds explanation, caveats, and transitions while
  leaving obsolete or duplicate material intact.
- **Diagnostic:** a proposed fix adds prose but cannot identify what will be replaced,
  moved, merged, or deleted.
- **Positive example:** the repair consolidates the authoritative statement, removes
  duplicates, and adds only the bridge still needed.
- **Negative example:** another explanatory paragraph is appended beside conflicting
  or redundant versions.
- **False-positive boundary:** addition alone is valid when a genuinely missing
  function is localized and no existing text competes with it.

## Scientifically precise but unreadable

- **Reader harm:** internal consistency masks an argument that cannot be followed in
  the intended reading order.
- **Diagnostic:** all definitions and conditions exist, but they appear in an order
  that forces reconstruction or repeated rereading.
- **Positive example:** precision is retained while premises, question, rationale, and
  technical detail are sequenced by dependency.
- **Negative example:** the dossier is declared clear because labels are consistent,
  despite the reader meeting consequences before premises.
- **False-positive boundary:** difficulty caused by irreducible subject matter is not
  a narrative defect when prerequisites are stated, definitions arrive on time, and
  no simpler faithful route exists.
