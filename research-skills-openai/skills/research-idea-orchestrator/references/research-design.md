# Research design considerations

Read when moving from a broad interest or dataset to a specific, defensible question.

## Find the contribution

Distinguish what is already established, what remains uncertain, and what the proposed study would change. A difference in setting, population, or method matters when it addresses a consequential limitation or tests whether prior findings travel.

Consider alternative explanations and evidence that would count against the idea. Avoid describing an area as unexplored merely because a limited search found little. State the scope of the evidence checked and what novelty remains uncertain.

## Connect the question to the study

Explain the path from accessible inputs through methods to interpretable outputs. Identify the target population or system, the comparison, and the outcome or phenomenon of interest where applicable.

Separate causal, predictive, descriptive, and interpretive aims. An attractive dataset does not automatically identify a causal effect, and a prediction improvement does not automatically establish practical benefit.

Test whether measurement, sample size or information content, timing, access, and expertise are adequate for the intended inference. An unresolved feasibility issue should lead to a smaller study, a pilot, or an explicit dependency.

## Compare choices

Compare scientific value and uncertainty alongside cost, duration, available expertise, and the consequences of failure. Do not manufacture numerical scores or precision for qualitative judgments. If options share the same evidence or failure mode, make that dependence visible.

Describe the smallest useful next investigation that could discriminate among the options. Keep a viable modest contribution visible even when a more ambitious direction is attractive.
